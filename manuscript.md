## Abstract

This dissertation asks how Canadian constitutional practice might better account for treaty relationships as continuing relationships among Indigenous Nations and the Crown, rather than treating treaties chiefly as historical instruments or questions to be resolved through state institutions alone. It brings together four fields that are often considered separately: treaty constitutionalism, Indigenous–Crown relations, Canadian federalism, and the integrity of library and archival records.

The project will examine how constitutional texts, treaty records, legislation, court decisions, administrative files, and community-authorized accounts are created, described, interpreted, and used. Its central premise is methodological rather than a conclusion: the conditions under which a record is preserved and made legible can shape which accounts of a treaty relationship enter public and legal decision-making. The project will test that premise through a bounded, comparative set of treaty and institutional case studies to be selected with attention to region, treaty-making context, available records, and the research permissions and protocols of the relevant communities.

The study will combine historical and doctrinal analysis with archival research and, only where appropriately authorized and ethically approved, interviews or other community-directed methods. It will distinguish the Crown as a continuing legal and political institution from the many governments and offices through which Crown authority is exercised. It will also avoid treating Indigenous Nations as interchangeable or assuming that state-held records are complete, neutral, or sufficient evidence of a Nation's law and account of its own treaty.

The intended contribution is a framework for analyzing treaty relationships across jurisdictions and records, together with a practical archival-integrity protocol for researchers and collecting institutions. That protocol will address provenance, description, access, consent, restrictions, correction, citation, and community authority over sensitive knowledge. The protocol is a proposal for testing and refinement, not a substitute for any Nation's own laws, policies, or research protocols. No empirical findings are claimed in this working edition.

**Keywords:** treaty constitutionalism; Indigenous–Crown relations; federalism; archives; libraries; provenance; access protocols; Canada.

# 1. Manuscript status and research commitments

### 1.1 What this document is

This edition establishes a research question, a proposed structure, a page budget, and a research protocol for a dissertation provisionally titled *The Crown as a Constitutional Relationship*. It is designed to guide a full research and writing process. It does not report completed archival research, interviews, community consultations, or settled conclusions.

The title is a proposition to investigate, not a claim that the Crown is a single, consensual, or culturally neutral relationship. In Canadian public law, “the Crown” can refer to a continuing legal institution and can be expressed through federal, provincial, territorial, and other state actors. Indigenous Nations have their own laws, political orders, histories, and accounts of their relationships with the Crown. The dissertation must make those differences visible rather than collapsing them into a single national story.

### 1.2 Research questions

**Primary question**

How should Canadian constitutional analysis understand treaty relationships between Indigenous Nations and the Crown when those relationships are mediated by federal institutions and by archival records whose creation, custody, description, and access are uneven?

**Secondary questions**

1. What concepts of treaty, constitutional authority, and continuing relationship are advanced in selected Indigenous legal and political scholarship, treaty histories, legal texts, and community-authorized sources?
2. How do courts and governments translate treaty relationships into doctrines, jurisdictional rules, and administrative practice?
3. In what ways do the provenance, arrangement, description, access conditions, and gaps of selected archival collections shape what researchers and institutions can responsibly say about those relationships?
4. What duties and practical steps should public libraries, archives, and researchers consider when preserving or providing access to records concerning Indigenous Nations and treaties?
5. Which parts of a proposed archival-integrity protocol can be stated as general professional practice, and which must remain specific to a Nation's own laws, instructions, or agreements?

### 1.3 Scope and limits

The project concerns selected treaty relationships and records in Canada; it will not attempt a comprehensive history of all treaties, Nations, or Crown interactions. Case studies remain to be chosen. Their selection must be justified before substantive comparison and must not be based solely on the convenience of state archival holdings.

The work will distinguish among:

- historical and contemporary uses of “Crown” in law and government;
- treaties as distinct agreements and relationships, rather than a single uniform category;
- judicial interpretation, executive and legislative practice, and Indigenous legal orders;
- a record's existence, its evidentiary value, and the authority to disclose or reuse it;
- publicly available material and knowledge subject to community, cultural, personal, or legal restrictions.

This dissertation will not purport to speak for any Indigenous Nation or to determine the meaning of a treaty on a Nation's behalf. It will not infer consent from silence, public availability, archival custody, or the absence of a restriction in a catalogue. It will not substitute OCAP® principles, Traditional Knowledge labels, or any generic research ethics framework for a community's own law or protocol. Those resources may be relevant where their custodians or rights-holders identify them as applicable.

### 1.4 Working propositions to test

These propositions guide inquiry; they are not findings:

- Constitutional analysis may be incomplete if it considers state doctrine without attending to the distinct Indigenous laws and treaty accounts engaged by the dispute.
- “Federalism” can describe both the allocation of authority within Canadian governments and the practical fragmentation of Crown decision-making across offices and orders of government.
- Archival description and access arrangements can enable or constrain later interpretation, but records must be assessed collection by collection and in relation to their custodians and creators.
- A responsible research method must treat source integrity and permission as substantive research questions, not merely citation mechanics.

# 2. Introduction: the constitutional relationship and the record

## 2.1 Problem and purpose

Treaties sit at the intersection of law, history, politics, and continuing relationships. Canadian constitutional analysis often approaches them through legal categories such as treaty rights, title, consultation, jurisdiction, and the honour of the Crown. Those categories matter, but they do not exhaust the meaning or authority of a treaty for the Indigenous Nations who made, maintain, and interpret their own agreements. Nor do they resolve how the state should act when responsibilities are divided among departments, legislatures, courts, and levels of government.

The same difficulty appears in the documentary record. A researcher may encounter a treaty through a Crown clerk's journal, a treaty text, a map, a court exhibit, a community-held copy, or a modern catalogue description. Each source has a history. It may record one participant's words, reflect an interpreter's choices, be incomplete, have been separated from related materials, or be held under restrictions that are not apparent in a digitized image. The documentary record is therefore not a transparent window onto a relationship. It is evidence produced and preserved within relationships of power and responsibility.

This dissertation proposes to study these issues together. Its focus is not simply whether archives contain relevant material, but how constitutional actors and researchers decide what counts as evidence, whose account is treated as authoritative, and what responsibilities follow from custody and use. Connecting constitutional analysis with archival practice can clarify both the possibilities and the limits of the records on which legal and historical arguments rely.

## 2.2 The analytical problem

Three analytical separations motivate the project.

First, treaty discussion can become detached from relationship. A treaty may be framed as an event in the past or as a source of rights interpreted by contemporary courts, while the continuing political and legal relationship is less visible. The project asks what is gained and lost when constitutional analysis starts from relationship and obligation rather than from the state institution's account alone.

Second, jurisdictional analysis can obscure institutional responsibility. Canadian federalism allocates powers among governments, but a person or Nation encountering the Crown may experience a connected sequence of decisions by different offices. The dissertation will examine whether fragmentation affects the recognition, implementation, or remedying of treaty commitments. It will not assume that every difficulty is caused by federalism or that a single Crown actor can answer for every decision.

Third, legal arguments can treat archival records as stable objects, despite the conditions of their production and custody. A document's preservation can make it discoverable, while its description can misidentify people or places, privilege an institutional vocabulary, or omit relationships among records. Conversely, restrictions can protect sensitive knowledge and community authority. Access is not an uncomplicated good; decisions require context, accountability, and appropriate authority.

## 2.3 Significance

The proposed contribution is a framework that links constitutional reasoning, institutional practice, and documentary responsibility without treating them as interchangeable. It is intended to assist legal and historical researchers, public institutions, and archivists in asking:

- what relationship is being described and by whom;
- which legal and political orders are relevant;
- how government responsibilities are distributed in practice;
- what a record can and cannot establish;
- who has authority to authorize access, description, interpretation, and reuse;
- what correction or accountability mechanisms are available when a record or its description causes harm.

The practical output will be a protocol that institutions can adapt in collaboration with relevant Nations and communities. Its usefulness will depend on that collaboration. It should not be published as a universal standard before review by those whose records and knowledge it concerns.

## 2.4 Terms and use

**Crown:** A term used in Canadian law and public administration for a continuing state institution and its representatives. The dissertation will specify the relevant actor, jurisdiction, and context rather than use “the Crown” as shorthand for a single decision-maker.

**Treaty relationship:** A term used provisionally for the continuing legal, political, and relational dimensions of a particular treaty as understood by its parties and relevant rights-holders. It does not imply that all parties use the same concepts or that the researcher can settle disputed meanings.

**Archival integrity:** In this project, the responsible preservation, description, access, citation, and reuse of records, with attention to provenance, context, restrictions, correction, and the authority of the people and communities represented.

**Indigenous Nation and community:** Terms will follow the self-identification and preferred terminology of the specific peoples and governing bodies involved. No single term will be presumed to fit all contexts.

## 2.5 Structure of the dissertation

Chapter 3 will establish the analytical framework and review relevant scholarship. Chapter 4 will contextualize treaty-making, Crown authority, and the production of records without suggesting one uniform history. Chapter 5 will present comparative case studies selected through transparent criteria and community engagement. Chapter 6 will examine how federal institutions translate obligations into practice. Chapter 7 will set out the archival-integrity analysis and protocol. Chapter 8 will synthesize the findings and offer appropriately bounded recommendations. The conclusion will assess the contribution and limits of the study.

# 3. Concepts and literature: treaty constitutionalism and federalism

## 3.1 Purpose of the review

The literature review will bring Indigenous legal scholarship into conversation with constitutional law, treaty history, federalism, and archival studies. It will not use Canadian state law as the sole framework against which Indigenous legal orders are measured. It will identify differences in method and authority, including who is entitled to interpret a treaty and what forms of knowledge may be shared.

## 3.2 Treaty constitutionalism

This section will ask whether and how treaties can be understood as constitutional commitments that structure relationships and obligations over time. It will distinguish this inquiry from the narrower question of how courts classify treaty rights under the Constitution Act, 1982. It will consider the Royal Proclamation of 1763 and section 35 in their legal and historical contexts, while avoiding the assumption that either instrument captures the entirety of a particular Nation's law or treaty understanding.

The analysis will examine competing accounts of treaty-making, including oral histories and Indigenous legal scholarship where authorized and appropriately contextualized. It will identify whose interpretation is being reported, the limits of the source, and any translation or transcription issues. Concepts such as consent, reciprocity, peace, land, jurisdiction, and continuity will not be treated as self-evident or universal.

## 3.3 Indigenous legal orders and constitutional dialogue

This section will review work that describes Indigenous laws and political orders on their own terms and examines their interaction with Canadian institutions. It will consider the methodological question of how a scholar can accurately discuss a legal order without claiming authority to speak for its knowledge-holders. The chapter will identify the sources, protocols, and forms of review needed for each case study.

## 3.4 Canadian federalism and Crown responsibility

The federalism analysis will distinguish constitutional division of powers from practical administrative coordination. It will examine how legislative authority, program delivery, negotiation, records management, and remedies are distributed, and how that distribution affects treaty-related decisions. The study will not presume that a division of powers automatically divides or extinguishes a relationship; that is an issue to be examined in the context of specific legal sources and facts.

## 3.5 Archival theory and public memory

Archival scholarship provides tools for examining provenance, original order, appraisal, description, custody, access, and the institutional production of public memory. The dissertation will ask how these concepts apply where state archives hold records about Indigenous Nations, where records are held by Nations or families, and where access or use is governed by community protocols or agreements.

The review will also address the limits of “open access” as a universal remedy. Digitization can reduce physical barriers, but it can also detach an item from its context, expose sensitive information at scale, or make reuse possible beyond the conditions under which a record was shared. The project will distinguish discovery from permission and preservation from unrestricted publication.

## 3.6 Synthesis and analytical framework

The chapter will conclude with a four-part framework:

1. **Relationship:** identify the parties, the specific treaty context, and the distinct accounts of continuing obligation.
2. **Authority:** identify the legal and political orders and the authority each source or institution claims.
3. **Institutional pathway:** trace how decisions and responsibilities move across Crown offices and jurisdictions.
4. **Record conditions:** assess how evidence was made, preserved, described, accessed, and authorized for use.

Each case study will be analyzed through all four parts. Where evidence is unavailable or its use is not authorized, the gap will be recorded as a limit rather than filled with speculation.

# 4. Historical contexts: treaties, Crown authority, and record-making

## 4.1 A contextual, not comprehensive, history

This chapter will provide only the historical context needed for the selected case studies. Canada contains distinct treaty-making histories and relationships; a national overview must not imply a single treaty experience. The final chapter will define geographic, temporal, and political scope and explain whose histories are included and why.

## 4.2 Crown authority over time

The chapter will trace changes in the legal and administrative forms through which Crown authority was asserted and exercised in the selected contexts. It will distinguish proclamations, legislation, constitutional instruments, negotiated agreements, administrative practice, and judicial doctrine. Each will be treated as a different kind of source, with attention to its creator, audience, purpose, and limits.

## 4.3 Treaty-making and interpretation

For each selected treaty context, the dissertation will examine the available evidence of negotiation, interpretation, implementation, and later disagreement. Written texts will be read alongside other sources where available and authorized. The absence of a written record will not be taken to prove that an event or obligation did not exist; nor will an oral account be detached from the community and protocols that give it meaning.

## 4.4 The making of the documentary record

This section will map the creation and custody of records likely to inform each case study. It will ask:

- Who created each record, and for what purpose?
- What languages, translation practices, and recording conventions were involved?
- Which materials were retained, transferred, copied, or omitted?
- What relationships among records are obscured by their current arrangement?
- Who now holds the material, and what restrictions or agreements govern it?

The resulting map will distinguish the archive's holdings from the broader record of a relationship. It will identify community-held sources only where their custodians have authorized their inclusion.

## 4.5 Periodization and change

Rather than impose a single national timeline, the chapter will use periodization appropriate to each case study. It will examine moments of legal or administrative change only where relevant to the research questions and supported by sources. Any comparison across cases will state where contexts differ and why a comparison remains analytically useful.

# 5. Treaty relationships in practice: comparative case studies

## 5.1 Case selection

No case study is preselected in this edition. Selection will occur after a scoping review and consultation with relevant communities and institutions. Proposed criteria are:

- a clear connection to the primary research question;
- meaningful variation in treaty-making context and government institutions;
- sufficient and diverse source material, without equating abundance of state records with importance;
- feasibility of obtaining permissions and complying with community research protocols;
- a research design that does not impose disproportionate demands on communities;
- a defensible basis for comparison and a clear statement of what cannot be compared.

At least two cases will be needed for a comparative design. The final number will depend on scope and authorization, not a predetermined quota.

## 5.2 Case study template

Each case chapter will use the following sequence, adapted to local context:

1. **Context and parties:** identify the Nation or Nations, Crown actors, treaty context, and terminology preferred by participants.
2. **Sources and authority:** describe the evidence used, its provenance, any restrictions, and the authority under which it is included.
3. **Treaty account:** present relevant accounts with attribution and context, distinguishing direct evidence from secondary interpretation.
4. **Constitutional and legal treatment:** analyze applicable constitutional texts, statutes, decisions, and legal scholarship.
5. **Institutional pathway:** trace the government offices and jurisdictions involved in the issue under study.
6. **Record analysis:** assess how records were created, arranged, described, accessed, and cited.
7. **Limits and unresolved questions:** identify disputed interpretations, gaps, withheld material, and matters outside the researcher's authority.
8. **Comparative contribution:** state which dimensions can responsibly be compared with other cases and which must remain distinct.

## 5.3 Comparative method

Comparison will be organized around questions, not a ranking of Nations or treaties. The study may compare how responsibilities are distributed, what sources are treated as authoritative, or what archival access and correction mechanisms exist. It will not rank the validity or completeness of different treaty accounts.

## 5.4 Reporting disagreements

Where accounts differ, the dissertation will describe the disagreement, attribute claims, and identify the relevant sources and limits. It will not manufacture consensus or resolve a matter that belongs to the parties, a Nation's governing institutions, or a competent legal process. A Nation's decision not to participate or disclose information will be respected and will not be framed as a deficiency in the research.

# 6. Federal institutions and the implementation of treaty obligations

## 6.1 From constitutional allocation to lived administration

This chapter will trace how selected treaty-related responsibilities move through government institutions. It will consider the difference between the formal distribution of legislative authority and the operational realities of policy, funding, negotiation, consultation, records management, and dispute resolution.

## 6.2 Institutional mapping

For each case, the research will create an institutional map identifying:

- the federal, provincial, territorial, and other public bodies involved;
- the legal or policy basis each body invokes;
- the sequence of decisions and points of transfer;
- the procedures for coordination and for resolving disagreement;
- the office responsible for maintaining and disclosing relevant records;
- the available routes for review, correction, or remedy.

The map will be based on public legal and administrative sources and, if authorized, additional records or interviews. It will distinguish documented process from inference.

## 6.3 Accountability and continuity

The chapter will investigate how governments maintain continuity of responsibility when ministers, departments, policies, or programs change. It will examine whether institutional handoffs make treaty commitments difficult to identify or implement in the selected contexts. It will not presume that one organizational model will suit all relationships.

## 6.4 Remedies and limits

Legal remedies, political processes, negotiated mechanisms, and community-led processes will be described in context. The dissertation will distinguish a remedy available in law from one that is accessible or regarded as appropriate by the affected Nation. Recommendations will be limited to matters supported by the study and within the relevant institution's authority.

# 7. Library and archival integrity: evidence, description, and access

## 7.1 Why archival integrity belongs in the study

Records do not merely preserve information; they are created, selected, organized, described, and accessed within institutional and social relationships. A catalogue entry may be the only context a researcher sees before using a document. A digitized item may circulate independently of its restrictions or related materials. These features can affect legal and historical interpretation.

This chapter will not claim that every archival description is harmful or that every record should be closed. It will examine the actual practices and records in the selected cases and make recommendations proportionate to the evidence.

## 7.2 Proposed archival-integrity protocol

The following protocol is a starting point for research and institutional discussion. It must be adapted through dialogue with relevant Nations, rights-holders, record creators, custodians, and applicable law. It does not replace a Nation's own protocols or a binding agreement.

### A. Before collection or use

1. Identify the record's creator, current custodian, provenance, and known transfer history.
2. Determine whether the material is public, restricted, culturally sensitive, personally identifying, or subject to an agreement or community protocol.
3. Contact the appropriate rights-holders or governing body through a respectful, locally appropriate process; do not presume a single contact represents all interests.
4. Define the proposed purpose, scope, audience, storage, quotation, digitization, and future reuse before seeking access.
5. Obtain institutional ethics review and legal guidance where required, while recognizing that institutional approval is not community authorization.

### B. Description and context

1. Preserve existing descriptions and document any changes, with dates and responsible editors.
2. Use community-preferred names and terms where authorized; retain necessary historical terminology as attributed source language, not as an endorsed descriptor.
3. Avoid adding sensitive personal, cultural, or location details solely to improve searchability.
4. Link related records and explain known gaps, translation, copying, or provenance issues.
5. Provide a clear route for a community or individual to request correction, contextualization, restriction, or review.

### C. Access and handling

1. Separate discovery from permission to view, quote, reproduce, or redistribute.
2. Apply restrictions consistently and explain their basis where disclosure is lawful and appropriate.
3. Use proportionate safeguards for sensitive data, including controlled access or delayed release where agreed and legally supportable.
4. Do not infer consent from a record's online availability, past publication, or deposit in a public institution.
5. Record access decisions, permissions, expiry dates, and conditions in a way future staff can find and honor.

### D. Citation, interpretation, and reuse

1. Cite the repository, collection, identifier, creator, date, and version or access date as applicable.
2. Distinguish a record's own claims from the researcher's interpretation and from a community's authorized account.
3. Preserve uncertainty and disagreement; do not silently normalize spellings, remove context, or present a transcription as an original.
4. Follow agreed attribution, review, and reuse conditions, including restrictions on images, audio, oral histories, and community knowledge.
5. Reassess whether continued online availability remains appropriate when circumstances, permissions, or community direction change.

### E. Correction, return, and accountability

1. Maintain a documented process for correction, contextual notes, access review, and complaints.
2. Notify affected parties of material changes where feasible and appropriate.
3. Develop return, repatriation, or shared-custody processes with the relevant community rather than assuming institutional ownership settles the question.
4. Assign responsibility and timelines for reviewing restrictions and agreements.
5. Publish an annual account of protocol use in a form that does not itself disclose restricted information.

## 7.3 Evaluation questions

The case studies will assess the protocol against these questions:

- Can users discover a record without its sensitive content being exposed?
- Can a community correct a harmful or inaccurate description?
- Are restrictions visible to staff and users over time?
- Are permissions specific to the proposed use and audience?
- Is provenance sufficiently documented to support responsible interpretation?
- Does the institution recognize community authority and provide a practical route for direction or review?
- Are digitization and reuse governed as separate decisions?

The evaluation will report both effective practices and unresolved barriers. It will not score a community's willingness to provide access.

# 8. A relationship-centred framework and institutional recommendations

## 8.1 Synthesis

This chapter will bring the four analytical dimensions together. For each case, it will explain how the relationship is framed, which sources and authorities are relied upon, how government responsibilities are distributed, and what record conditions affect the analysis. It will identify recurring patterns only where evidence across cases supports them.

## 8.2 Recommendations for public institutions

Recommendations will be developed after the research, but may address:

- transparent institutional maps and named points of responsibility;
- durable coordination and handoff procedures for treaty-related records and decisions;
- community-directed review of descriptions, access conditions, and digitization plans;
- resourcing for Indigenous-led archival stewardship and shared description;
- preservation of provenance, translation history, restrictions, and permission records;
- accessible processes for correction, contextualization, and reconsideration of access;
- researcher training that treats permission and source authority as core method.

These are candidate areas, not final recommendations. Each must be evaluated against jurisdiction, evidence, community direction, cost, and unintended effects.

## 8.3 Contribution to scholarship and practice

The anticipated scholarly contribution is an integrated method for examining treaty constitutionalism and the documentary conditions of constitutional knowledge. The anticipated practice contribution is a protocol that institutions can test and revise with communities. The dissertation will state clearly whether the evidence supports these contributions and where it does not.

# 9. Conclusion: limits, contributions, and next research

The conclusion will answer the primary research question in terms supported by the case studies. It will distinguish conclusions about Canadian institutions from conclusions about any particular treaty relationship or Nation. It will summarize methodological lessons, identify limitations in the available and authorized record, and explain how those limits shape the claims.

The conclusion will not treat absent evidence as evidence of absence, nor treat institutional holdings as a complete account of treaty history. It will identify questions for further research and any protocol elements that require additional community review before adoption. The final text should state what remains unknown, what could not be accessed, and what future researchers must not assume.
